Every seat in a chit fund is an option. What is yours worth?

In a Tamil chit, friends pool money every month and bid for who takes the pot. Taking it early is borrowing; taking it late is saving. This project prices that choice with the maths used for stock options.

Play a chit How it works

How a chit works

Ten friends, ₹1,000 each a month, ten months. Every month runs the same three steps.

  1. 1

    Everyone pays in

    Ten members pay ₹1,000 each.

    ₹10,000 in the pot

  2. 2

    Bid for the pot

    Members who haven't had a pot yet bid how much of it they'll give up. Priya gives up the most, ₹2,000.

    ₹8,000 to Priya

  3. 3

    Share what she gave up

    The organiser keeps ₹500 commission. The other ₹1,500 is split ten ways.

    ₹850 is all anyone pays this month

Win early and you've borrowed: you get a lump sum now and pay it back over the coming months.

Win late and you've saved: you paid in first, collected the shared-out money along the way, and get the pot at the end.

The catch: you can only win once. Waiting might get you the pot cheaper later, but if you suddenly need money you'll wish you'd taken it. Choosing when to use a one-time right is exactly the problem an American option solves.

Play a chit

You're one of ten members. Each month the bidding climbs; drop out when the price is too high, or stay in and take the pot. Life will throw an emergency or two at you. At the end, see how you did against the model.

Checked against real chits

Two things can't be observed: how often members need cash and what outside loans cost them. Both were tuned so the model reproduces real bids from a Tamil Nadu chit company (40 members, ₹10,000 pot), studied by Klonner and Rai.

Gets right

  • Rates average 14.5% a year, inside the 14–24% reported.
  • The yield peaks in month 16 of 40, early to mid tenure.
  • A 30% cap lowers early bids, as in 1993.

Gets wrong

  • After the cap, 95% of early bids sit at it; real chits had 72%.
  • It predicts late bids rise after the cap; really they fell, just less.

The model's members are identical and never default. Real members differ and some default, which is the likely reason for both misses.

How it was built

One tested Python package does all the maths, and this page runs the same code in your browser.

Seat value and best bids
Backward induction from the last month, as for an American option on a tree. Checked against a brute-force search over all 1,024 strategies in a small chit.
Longstaff–Schwartz
Learns the same values from 50,000 simulated futures and agrees with the backward induction. A smooth polynomial regression, the textbook choice, loses value here because the best rule jumps when the first rival needs cash.
Equilibrium
Everyone bids up to the price at which winning and waiting are equally good. Best responses are iterated until they stop changing; three different starting points give the same answer and nobody can gain by deviating. The game's rivals follow this.
Rates
The number of members in need is a Markov chain, so the rate curves are computed exactly, then confirmed by simulation.
Bidding advice
A hidden-Markov filter reads past winning bids to infer how many members need cash.

Limits: members are identical, know how many others need cash, and never default. Calibration uses two published figures from one company, not bid-by-bid records.
