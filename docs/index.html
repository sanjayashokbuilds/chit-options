What is a seat in a chit fund worth?

In a Tamil chit, or சீட்டு, every member pays in each month and one member takes the whole pot. They bid for it: whoever will give up the most of the pot wins, and what they give up is shared among everyone.

Taking the pot early is borrowing; taking it late is saving. Each seat is a one-time right to choose when, which is exactly what an American option is. This project prices that right.

Play through one simulated 20-member chit to see how it works.

Why a seat behaves like an option

Every month you hold a seat, you choose: take the pot now, at whatever discount wins the auction, or wait. Waiting keeps the chance to win later and more cheaply, but if a cash emergency arrives while you wait, money now becomes worth much more to you.

That trade-off is the same one a holder of an American option faces, so the same tools apply. Starting from the last month and working backwards gives the value of waiting in every situation (dynamic programming). Simulating thousands of futures and learning the value of waiting by regression gives the same answer in a different way (Longstaff–Schwartz). Then everyone bids this way at once, and the bids are iterated until nobody can do better by changing theirs (an equilibrium).

Economists, above all Stefan Klonner, have modelled chit auctions before. What is new here is pricing the seat with option methods, measuring how its value responds to each setting, and asking when in a chit's life loans are most expensive.

When are chit loans most expensive?

Each winning bid implies an interest rate: the price of having the pot now rather than at the end. Practitioners say these rates peak a quarter to a third of the way through a chit. Change the settings to see when the model agrees.

Yield to end: the average rate a winner pays from that month to the end. Forward rate: the price of getting the pot one month earlier. Both are averages over all the ways the chit could unfold, computed exactly rather than simulated.

Checked against real chits

Two things about members can't be observed: how often they need cash and what outside loans cost them. Both were tuned so the model reproduces bids from a chit company in Tamil Nadu studied by Klonner and Rai: 40 members, 40 months, a ₹10,000 pot.

What the model gets right

  • Rates average about 14.5% a year, inside the 14–24% reported for these chits.
  • The yield peaks in month 16 of 40, 40% of the way through: early to mid tenure.
  • A 30% cap lowers early bids, as it did in 1993.

What it gets wrong

  • After the cap it puts 95% of early bids at the cap; real chits had 72%.
  • It predicts late bids rise after the cap. In reality they fell, just less than early bids.

Real members differ from each other and some default. The model's members are identical and always pay, which is the likely reason for both misses.

How it was built

Everything runs on one tested Python package, the same code this page runs in your browser.

  • Seat value and best bids. Backward induction over (month, own need, number of others in need), checked against a brute-force search over all 1,024 strategies in a small chit.
  • Longstaff–Schwartz. Learns the same values from 50,000 simulated futures; agrees with the dynamic programme within simulation error. A smooth polynomial regression, the textbook choice, loses value here because the best rule jumps when the first rival needs cash.
  • Equilibrium. Everyone bids up to the price at which winning and waiting are equally good; best responses are iterated until they stop changing. The same answer is reached from three different starting points, and nobody can gain by deviating.
  • Rates. The number of members in need is a Markov chain, so the rate curves are computed exactly and then confirmed by simulation.
  • Bidding advice. A hidden-Markov filter reads past winning bids to infer how many members need cash.

Limits worth knowing: members are identical, know how many others need cash, and never default. Calibration uses two published figures from one company, not bid-by-bid records.
